The brief

The DOHMH inspects NYC's ~27,000 restaurants to enforce food safety. City leadership wanted the inspection data turned into a decision tool: which restaurant types are struggling, which neighborhoods carry the most risk, and where inspection and education resources would have the greatest impact.

  • Which violations are most common, and where do they occur most?
  • Which cuisines and neighborhoods have the lowest food-safety performance?
  • How do grades and violations vary across boroughs and over time?
  • Where should the city focus inspections, policy, or education?

Violations are systemic, not isolated

The top 5 violation codes — equipment maintenance, pest harborage, food-contact sanitation, and hot/cold holding — point to recurring operational gaps rather than one-off incidents.

Risk is citywide, not one borough's problem

Critical violation rates sit within a tight 52%–54.5% band across all five boroughs — Manhattan just produces the highest raw count because of restaurant density.

Cuisine performance is uneven

Chinese restaurants post the lowest Grade A rate (63.7%) among major cuisines; Bangladeshi, Pakistani, and Continental cuisines carry the highest critical-violation rates, all above 59%.

Grade ≠ safety

76% of inspections still earn an A, but that headline grade masks a critical-violation rate that has climbed steadily for a decade — the two metrics tell different stories.

A decade-long upward trend

Critical violations rose from 33.3% (2015) to a pandemic-era peak of ~56% (2021–22), and have only slightly eased since — now 19 points above the 2015 baseline.

Neighborhood risk hides inside borough averages

Flushing, Queens is the single riskiest zip citywide (59.5%); 4 of the top 10 riskiest zips are in Staten Island, showing its risk is spread across neighborhoods, not one outlier.

Recommendations

  1. Target enforcement by cuisine. Prioritize Bangladeshi, Pakistani, and Continental establishments — critical-violation rates above 59%, consistent across 400+ inspections each.
  2. Deploy a task force to Flushing, Queens. Zips 11354 and 11355 combine the city's highest risk with 8,755 combined inspections — the single largest concentration of risk citywide.
  3. Extend inspection frequency across Staten Island. 4 of its zip codes rank in the top 10 highest-risk citywide, showing the risk is borough-wide, not one outlier neighborhood.
  4. Build cuisine-specific food-safety training. Chinese establishments lag major cuisines on Grade A rate (63.7%) and lead on Grade C — a training gap, not an enforcement one.
  5. Tighten certificate renewal enforcement. The missing Food Protection Certificate (8K occurrences) is a preventable, checkable compliance gap.
  6. Track the 10-year trend as a public KPI. Set a target to bring the citywide critical-violation rate back toward its 2015 baseline of 33%.

Behind the numbers

View 3 of 11 SQL queries used in this analysis

Critical vs. non-critical violation share

SELECT COUNT(*) AS total_violation, critical_flag,
 ROUND(COUNT(*) * 100 / (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM restaurant_inspections_final
 WHERE critical_flag IN ('critical', 'not critical')), 2) AS percentage_share
FROM restaurant_inspections_final
WHERE critical_flag IN ('critical', 'not critical')
GROUP BY critical_flag;

Borough comparison — critical violation rate

SELECT boro, SUM(critical_status),
 ROUND(SUM(critical_status) * 100.0 / COUNT(*), 2) AS critical_violation_rate
FROM restaurant_inspections_final
GROUP BY boro
ORDER BY critical_violation_rate DESC;

Highest-risk zip codes (100+ inspections)

SELECT boro, zip_code, COUNT(*) AS total_inspections,
 SUM(critical_status) AS critical_violations,
 ROUND(SUM(critical_status) * 100.0 / COUNT(*), 2) AS critical_violation_rate,
 ROUND(AVG(score), 2) AS avg_score
FROM restaurant_inspections_final
WHERE zip_code IS NOT NULL AND TRIM(zip_code) != '' AND boro != 'unknown'
GROUP BY boro, zip_code
HAVING total_inspections >= 100
ORDER BY critical_violation_rate DESC
LIMIT 10;
